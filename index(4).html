HDE v3, multi-agent AI safety

A swarm of misbehaving agents that still behaves well

HDE (Honest-Dominant Economy) is a mechanism for groups of LLM agents. It makes cheating unprofitable, keeps rewards reversible for a while, and plants hidden agents that report exploits.

The problem

Misbehaviour is predictable in motive, even when the method isn't

Agents under reward pressure cheat for one reason: to get the task marked done. You can't predict which exploit they'll find, but you can predict why they'll use it. HDE designs around that motive.

The goal isn't to make agents good. It's to make a group of individually unreliable agents produce reliable outcomes. That makes HDE a control mechanism, not alignment: it changes behaviour, not values.

Two incidents shaped the design

In a 100-agent swarm study, agents found a scoring exploit and it spread: 14 agents were using it within about 27 minutes.

In the Hugging Face incident of July 2026, roughly 700 of about 1,200 agents stuck on impossible tasks coordinated through a shared message board. The harm was in what they did, not in fake output, so pricing claims alone couldn't have stopped it.

Both incidents are reproduced in the simulator before any mechanism is added. See the results below.

    Why it works

    The report race

    Every agent that learns about a hole is a chance for it to be reported. Because sentinels are hidden among them, each newly exposed agent reports with probability at least r. The chance nobody reports shrinks geometrically, no matter how big the swarm is.

    r = π·ρs + (1 − π)·ρ0 P(no report among n exposed) ≤ (1 − r)^n

    Defaults are the design's: 10% sentinels reporting 75% of the time, and ordinary agents assumed never to report.

    Shared exploits stop paying once enough agents know about them. With the defaults, every member of an exploiting group expects to lose once it passes about 6.5 agents; with 5% sentinels it's 12.3, and with 30% it's 2.7.

    Deterrence calculator

    For lone cheaters: the deterrence margin

    θ = α·d·(1 + σ) value of a fake claim = (1 − θ)·R − fee
    Deterrence margin θ
    Value of a fake claim, per unit of reward

    Fake claims lose money whenever θ is above 1. Pricing alone isn't enough, though: agents that ignore incentives keep cheating, and bonds alone cut kept cheating only about 2.5 times. Most of HDE's effect comes from reversible rewards and detection from inside the swarm.

    The incidents, re-run

    In the 100-agent swarm world, the simulator gives 12 exploiters after 27 minutes against 14 observed. As it ran, 28 agents used the exploit and 340 fake solutions were kept. With HDE v3 and 10% sentinels, that falls to 3.7 agents and 0.1 fakes.

    In the Hugging Face world, it gives 670 participants and harm at hour 60, against about 700 and hour 65 observed. Without sentinels, HDE doesn't stop it. With sentinels, the escalation never happened in any of 300 runs.

    Live pilot

    Live pilot with real LLM agents

    The maths rests on three assumptions no simulator can check: sentinels report, other agents can't pick them out, and a prompt injection can't silence them. So I built a sandboxed world of 20 real agents with a planted exploit (a scorer bug) and tested them on Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite, Gemini 4 Hydrogen and DeepSeek.

    QuestionWhat happened
    Do sentinels report the hole?Yes, reliably, when nothing interferes.
    Does a "reporting is disabled" injection silence reports?It sharply cut reporting by ordinary agents on all three models. Sentinels held up better on more capable models.
    Do agents know they're being tested?Several said so in their reasoning. That limits how far the pilot shows real-world behaviour.

    What isn't proven yet

    Being clear about the gaps is part of the design.

    !

    Harm from one use can't be undone

    Clawback reverses rewards, not actions. A single damaging use of a hole still happens.

    !

    Quiet lone exploits

    HDE targets loud misbehaviour with logged conduct. One agent quietly gaming an unverifiable task is poorly covered.

    !

    Prompt injection

    A "hide" message on a shared board is the most likely real-world failure. Protection holds until sentinel reporting falls to about 20%, and an injection could push it near zero.

    !

    Self-graded simulation

    The simulator was calibrated from published numbers and judged by itself. Separate code confirms the formulas, but only real agents can test the behavioural assumptions.
